Shiji V: Biographies

Shiji 史记 · 247 BC · 秦庄襄王三年

Introduction

The Biography of Lü Buwei from King Zhuangxiang's death: Buwei as Chancellor of State and Second Father to the young king, his three thousand retainers and Master Lü's Spring and Autumn Annals, and the matter of Lao Ai and the Queen Dowager; and the Biography of Li Si from his birth at Shangcai to his arrival in Qin in this year, his counsel to King Zheng, the memorial against the expulsion of the guests, and his rise to Chancellor.

[§5] 史记（五：列传）

[§5] Shiji V: Biographies

吕不韦列传：庄襄王即位三年，薨，太子政立为王，尊吕不韦为相国，号称“仲父”。秦王年少，太后时时窃私通吕不韦。不韦家僮万人。

Biography of Lü Buwei: King Zhuangxiang died in the third year after his accession1; the Heir Apparent, Zheng, was set up as king, and he honoured Lü Buwei as Chancellor of State, styling him "Second Father2". The King of Qin was young, and the Queen Dowager from time to time had secret relations with Lü Buwei. Buwei's household slaves numbered ten thousand.

当是时，魏有信陵君，楚有春申君，赵有平原君，齐有孟尝君，皆下士喜宾客以相倾。吕不韦以秦之彊，羞不如，亦招致士，厚遇之，至食客三千人。是时诸侯多辩士，如荀卿之徒，著书布天下。吕不韦乃使其客人人著所闻，集论以为八览、六论、十二纪，二十馀万言。以为备天地万物古今之事，号曰吕氏春秋。布咸阳市门，悬千金其上，延诸侯游士宾客有能增损一字者予千金。

At this time Wei had Lord Xinling3, Chu had Lord Chunshen, Zhao had Lord Pingyuan and Qi had Lord Mengchang, all of whom humbled themselves before gentlemen and delighted in retainers, each striving to outdo the others. Lü Buwei, for all Qin's strength, was ashamed to fall short of them, and he too drew gentlemen to him and treated them generously, until his retainers reached three thousand. At that time there were many disputants among the Lords, such as Xun Qing and his kind, who wrote books and spread them over all under Heaven. Lü Buwei therefore had each of his guests write down what he had heard, and gathered their discourses into the Eight Surveys, Six Discourses and Twelve Records4, more than two hundred thousand words. Holding that it comprised the affairs of heaven and earth, of the myriad things and of ancient and modern times, he called it Master Lü's Spring and Autumn Annals5. He displayed it at the market gate of Xianyang6, hung a thousand jin of gold above it, and invited the travelling gentlemen and retainers of the Lords: whoever could add or take away a single character would be given the thousand jin.

始皇帝益壮，太后淫不止。吕不韦恐觉祸及己，乃私求大阴人嫪毐以为舍人，时纵倡乐，使毐以其阴关桐轮而行，令太后闻之，以啗太后。太后闻，果欲私得之。吕不韦乃进嫪毐，诈令人以腐罪告之。不韦又阴谓太后曰：“可事诈腐，则得给事中。”太后乃阴厚赐主腐者吏，诈论之，拔其须眉为宦者，遂得侍太后。太后私与通，绝爱之。有身，太后恐人知之，诈卜当避时，徙宫居雍。嫪毐常从，赏赐甚厚，事皆决於嫪毐。嫪毐家僮数千人，诸客求宦为嫪毐舍人千馀人。

As the First Emperor grew to manhood7, the Queen Dowager's licence did not cease. Lü Buwei, fearing that it would be found out and disaster fall on himself, privately sought out a man of great member, Lao Ai, and made him a household retainer; at times, letting the players and musicians loose, he had Ai walk with a wheel of tong wood8 hung on his member, and let the Queen Dowager hear of it, so as to bait her. The Queen Dowager heard, and as expected wished to have him in secret. Lü Buwei then presented Lao Ai, and had someone falsely accuse him of an offence meriting castration9. Buwei also said to the Queen Dowager in private: "If the castration can be feigned, he can be had as a palace attendant." The Queen Dowager then secretly made rich gifts to the officer in charge of castrations, who passed false sentence on him, plucked out his beard and eyebrows and made him a eunuch; and so he came to attend the Queen Dowager. She had secret relations with him and loved him beyond measure. She became pregnant and, fearing that it would be known, feigned a divination that she should avoid the season, and moved her palace to Yong10. Lao Ai was always in attendance, the rewards given him were very rich, and all matters were decided by Lao Ai. Lao Ai's household slaves numbered several thousand, and the guests who sought office by becoming Lao Ai's household retainers more than a thousand.

李斯列传：李斯者，楚上蔡人也。年少时，为郡小吏，见吏舍厕中鼠食不絜，近人犬，数惊恐之。斯入仓，观仓中鼠，食积粟，居大庑之下，不见人犬之忧。於是李斯乃叹曰：“人之贤不肖譬如鼠矣，在所自处耳！”

Biography of Li Si: Li Si was a man of Shangcai11 in Chu. In his youth he was a petty officer of the Commandery12. He saw that the rats in the privy of the officers' quarters ate filth and, being near men and dogs, were constantly startled and frightened by them. Then Si went into the granary and watched the rats there: they ate the stored grain, lived under the great eaves, and had no fear of men or dogs. At this Li Si sighed: "Whether a man is worthy or worthless is like the case of the rats: it lies in where he places himself!"

乃从荀卿学帝王之术。学已成，度楚王不足事，而六国皆弱，无可为建功者，欲西入秦。辞於荀卿曰：“斯闻得时无怠，今万乘方争时，游者主事。今秦王欲吞天下，称帝而治，此布衣驰骛之时而游说者之秋也。处卑贱之位而计不为者，此禽鹿视肉，人面而能强行者耳。故诟莫大於卑贱，而悲莫甚於穷困。久处卑贱之位，困苦之地，非世而恶利，自讬於无为，此非士之情也。故斯将西说秦王矣。”

He then studied the arts of emperors and kings under Xun Qing. When his studies were complete, he judged that the King of Chu was not worth serving and that the Six States were all weak, with no chance of achievement among them, and wished to go west into Qin. Taking leave of Xun Qing he said: "Si has heard that when one meets one's hour one must not be idle. Now the lords of ten thousand chariots are contending for their hour, and it is the travellers who have the management of affairs13. Now the King of Qin wishes to swallow all under Heaven and rule under the title of Emperor; this is the hour for commoners to gallop, and the harvest-time of the travelling persuaders14. One who sits in a humble and lowly station and makes no plan for himself is a beast staring at meat15, with a man's face and able only to walk upright. So there is no greater disgrace16 than lowliness, no greater sorrow than want. To stay long in a humble station and in straitened circumstances, carping at the age17 and hating gain, entrusting oneself to inaction: that is not the true feeling of a gentleman. Therefore Si will go west and persuade the King of Qin."

至秦，会庄襄王卒，李斯乃求为秦相文信侯吕不韦舍人；不韦贤之，任以为郎。李斯因以得说，说秦王曰：“胥人者，去其几也。成大功者，在因瑕衅而遂忍之。昔者秦穆公之霸，终不东并六国者，何也？诸侯尚众，周德未衰，故五伯迭兴，更尊周室。自秦孝公以来，周室卑微，诸侯相兼，关东为六国，秦之乘胜役诸侯，盖六世矣。今诸侯服秦，譬若郡县。夫以秦之彊，大王之贤，由灶上骚除，足以灭诸侯，成帝业，为天下一统，此万世之一时也。今怠而不急就，诸侯复彊，相聚约从，虽有黄帝之贤，不能并也。”秦王乃拜斯为长史，听其计，阴遣谋士赍持金玉以游说诸侯。诸侯名士可下以财者，厚遗结之；不肯者，利剑刺之。离其君臣之计，秦王乃使其良将随其後。秦王拜斯为客卿。

He arrived in Qin just as King Zhuangxiang died18. Li Si then sought to become a household retainer of the Chancellor of Qin, Marquis Wenxin Lü Buwei; Buwei thought him able and appointed him a Court Gentleman. Li Si thereby got his chance to speak, and said to the King of Qin: "The petty man is one who lets slip his moment19. Those who achieve great deeds do so by seizing on a flaw and being ruthless in following it through20. In former days, when Duke Mu of Qin was hegemon, why did he never in the end annex the Six States to the east? Because the Lords were still many and the virtue of Zhou had not yet declined, so the Five Hegemons rose in turn and each in turn honoured the house of Zhou. Since Duke Xiao of Qin, the house of Zhou has sunk low, the Lords have swallowed one another, east of the Pass there are six states, and Qin has ridden its victories and made the Lords its servants for six reigns21 now. Today the Lords submit to Qin as if they were its Commanderies and Counties. With Qin's strength and the Great King's worth, it would be like sweeping the dust off a stove22: enough to wipe out the Lords, complete the imperial task and bring all under Heaven under one rule. This is the one chance in ten thousand generations. If now you are idle and do not hasten to finish it, the Lords will grow strong again and gather to swear the Vertical Alliance, and though you had the worth of the Yellow Emperor you could not annex them." The King of Qin then appointed Si Senior Clerk, followed his plan, and secretly sent out schemers bearing gold and jade to work on the Lords. Those famous men of the Lords who could be brought down with wealth he bound to himself with rich gifts; those who would not, he stabbed with a sharp sword. Having laid his plans to divide rulers from ministers, the King of Qin then sent his good generals in their wake. The King of Qin appointed Si Guest Minister.

会韩人郑国来间秦，以作注溉渠，已而觉。秦宗室大臣皆言秦王曰：“诸侯人来事秦者，大抵为其主游间於秦耳，请一切逐客。”李斯议亦在逐中。斯乃上书曰：

It happened that Zheng Guo, a man of Han, came to spy on Qin by building an irrigation canal23, and after a time this was discovered. The royal clan and great ministers of Qin all said to the King of Qin: "The men of the Lords who come to serve Qin for the most part only travel here to spy on Qin for their masters; we ask that all guests be expelled without exception24." Li Si was counted among those to be expelled. Si then submitted a memorial25, saying:

臣闻吏议逐客，窃以为过矣。昔缪公求士，西取由余於戎，东得百里奚於宛，迎蹇叔於宋，来丕豹、公孙支於晋。此五子者，不产於秦，而缪公用之，并国二十，遂霸西戎。孝公用商鞅之法，移风易俗，民以殷盛，国以富彊，百姓乐用，诸侯亲服，获楚、魏之师，举地千里，至今治彊。惠王用张仪之计，拔三川之地，西并巴、蜀，北收上郡，南取汉中，包九夷，制鄢、郢，东据成皋之险，割膏腴之壤，遂散六国之从，使之西面事秦，功施到今。昭王得范睢，废穰侯，逐华阳，彊公室，杜私门，蚕食诸侯，使秦成帝业。此四君者，皆以客之功。由此观之，客何负於秦哉！向使四君卻客而不内，疏士而不用，是使国无富利之实而秦无彊大之名也。

"Your servant has heard that the officers are deliberating the expulsion of the guests; in my humble view this is a mistake. In former days Duke Mu26 sought gentlemen: in the west he took You Yu from the Rong, in the east he got Baili Xi27 from Wan, he welcomed Jian Shu28 from Song, and he brought Pi Bao and Gongsun Zhi29 from Jin. These five were not born in Qin, yet Duke Mu used them, annexed twenty states30 and became hegemon over the Western Rong. Duke Xiao used the laws of Shang Yang, changed the ways and altered the customs; the people prospered and multiplied, the state grew rich and strong, the hundred clans were glad to be employed and the Lords submitted in friendship; he captured the armies of Chu and Wei and took a thousand li of land, and to this day the state is well governed and strong. King Hui used the plans of Zhang Yi31: he took the lands of Sanchuan, in the west annexed Ba and Shu, in the north gathered in Shang Commandery32, in the south took Hanzhong33, encompassed the Nine Yi34 and brought Yan and Ying under control; in the east he seized the fastness of Chenggao35 and cut off the fat and fertile soil; and so he scattered the Vertical Alliance of the Six States and made them face west and serve Qin, and the benefit of his deeds reaches to this day. King Zhao got Fan Ju, deposed Marquis Rang and expelled Huayang36, strengthened the ducal house and barred the private gates, nibbled away37 at the Lords like a silkworm, and brought Qin to the imperial task. These four lords all owed their success to guests. Seen in this light, how have the guests failed Qin? Had the four lords rejected the guests and not admitted them, kept gentlemen at a distance and not used them, the state would have had none of the substance of wealth and profit, and Qin none of its name for strength and greatness.

今陛下致昆山之玉，有随、和之宝，垂明月之珠，服太阿之剑，乘纤离之马，建翠凤之旗，树灵鼍之鼓。此数宝者，秦不生一焉，而陛下说之，何也？必秦国之所生然後可，则是夜光之璧不饰朝廷，犀象之器不为玩好，郑、卫之女不充後宫，而骏良駃騠不实外厩，江南金锡不为用，西蜀丹青不为采。所以饰後宫充下陈娱心意说耳目者，必出於秦然後可，则是宛珠之簪，傅玑之珥，阿缟之衣，锦绣之饰不进於前，而随俗雅化佳冶窈窕赵女不立於侧也。夫击甕叩缶弹筝搏髀，而歌呼呜呜快耳者，真秦之声也；郑、卫、桑间、昭、虞、武、象者，异国之乐也。今弃击甕叩缶而就郑卫，退弹筝而取昭虞，若是者何也？快意当前，適观而已矣。今取人则不然。不问可否，不论曲直，非秦者去，为客者逐。然则是所重者在乎色乐珠玉，而所轻者在乎人民也。此非所以跨海内制诸侯之术也。

"Now Your Majesty obtains the jade of Kunshan38, possesses the treasures of Sui and He39, hangs up the bright-moon pearl, wears the sword Tai'e40, rides the horses of Xianli41, raises banners of kingfisher-phoenix plumes and sets up drums of magic alligator hide42. Of these several treasures Qin produces not one, yet Your Majesty delights in them: why? If only what the state of Qin produces will do, then the jade disc that shines by night would not adorn the court, vessels of rhinoceros horn and ivory would not be toys and pleasures, the women of Zheng and Wey would not fill the harem, and fine steeds and jueti43 would not stock the outer stables; the gold and tin of Jiangnan would not be used, nor the cinnabar and azurite of Western Shu44 for colouring. If what adorns the harem and fills the lower ranks45, delighting the heart and pleasing the eye and ear, must come from Qin before it will do, then hairpins of Wan pearls, ear-pendants set with seed-pearls46, robes of E gauze47 and ornaments of brocade and embroidery would not be brought before you, and the lovely, bewitching, graceful women of Zhao, elegant and refined after the fashion48, would not stand at your side. Striking pots and beating jars49, plucking the zither and slapping the thigh, and howling "wu, wu" to delight the ear: that is the true music of Qin; the music of Zheng, Wey and Sangjian, the Zhao, Yu, Wu and Xiang50, are the music of other countries. Now you cast aside the striking of pots and the beating of jars and take up Zheng and Wey, put away the plucking of the zither and take the Zhao and Yu: why so? Because they please you here and now and suit the eye, that is all. But in taking men you do not do so. You ask not whether they will do or not, you weigh not the crooked and the straight; whoever is not of Qin must go, whoever is a guest is expelled. So then what is prized is women, music, pearls and jade, and what is held light is the people. This is not the way to bestride all within the seas and control the Lords.

臣闻地广者粟多，国大者人众，兵彊则士勇。是以太山不让土壤，故能成其大；河海不择细流，故能就其深；王者不卻众庶，故能明其德。是以地无四方，民无异国，四时充美，鬼神降福，此五帝、三王之所以无敌也。今乃弃黔首以资敌国，卻宾客以业诸侯，使天下之士退而不敢西向，裹足不入秦，此所谓“藉寇兵而赍盗粮”者也。

"Your servant has heard that where the land is broad the grain is plentiful, where the state is great the people are many, and where the arms are strong the soldiers are brave. Thus Taishan51 does not refuse the clods of earth, and so can achieve its greatness; the rivers and seas do not pick and choose among the little streams, and so can achieve their depth; a true king does not reject the common multitude, and so can make his virtue shine. Thus the land has no four quarters and the people no foreign country; the four seasons are full and fair and the gods and spirits send down blessing: this is why the Five Emperors and the Three Kings had no rival. Now instead you cast off the black-headed people to supply52 the enemy states, and turn away retainers to build up the Lords, making the gentlemen of all under Heaven draw back and not dare to face west, binding their feet so that they do not enter Qin: this is what is called "lending arms to the bandit and carrying provisions to the thief53".

夫物不产於秦，可宝者多；士不产於秦，而原忠者众。今逐客以资敌国，损民以益雠，内自虚而外树怨於诸侯，求国无危，不可得也。

"Things not produced in Qin that may be treasured are many; gentlemen not born in Qin who wish to be loyal are numerous. Now to expel the guests to supply the enemy states, to lose your people to strengthen your foes, emptying yourself within and planting resentment among the Lords without, and then to seek a state free of danger: it cannot be done."

秦王乃除逐客之令，复李斯官，卒用其计谋。官至廷尉。二十馀年，竟并天下，尊主为皇帝，以斯为丞相。夷郡县城，销其兵刃，示不复用。使秦无尺土之封，不立子弟为王，功臣为诸侯者，使後无战攻之患。

The King of Qin then rescinded the order expelling the guests and restored Li Si to his office54, and in the end used his plans. He rose in office to Commandant of Justice. In twenty-odd years55 Qin at last annexed all under Heaven, honoured its ruler as Emperor, and made Si Chancellor. The Fortresses of the Commanderies and Counties were levelled and their weapons melted down, to show that they would not be used again. Qin was made to have not a foot of land in fief; no sons or brothers were set up as kings, no meritorious ministers as Lords, so that afterwards there should be no trouble of wars and attacks.

Terms

Heir Apparent / 太子n. the designated successor of a king.
Chancellor of State / 相国n. the chief minister, addressed by his full title.
Second Father / 仲父n. the style a ruler gave a minister honoured as a father, after Duke Huan of Qi's for Guan Zhong (Zhengyi).
household slaves / 家僮n. the bondsmen of a great house.
gentlemen / 士n. men of the gentleman class, from whom officers and retainers were drawn.
retainers / 食客; 宾客n. guests kept at a lord's table; the clients of a great house.
disputant / 辩士n. a master of argument; a travelling rhetorician.
the Lords / 诸侯npl. the rulers of the states, collectively; in this period the kings of the warring states and the lesser lords, as distinct from the Zhou king.
jin of gold / 金n. the unit in which sums of gold are reckoned: a yi 镒 under the Warring States and Qin, a jin 斤 under Han.
household retainer / 舍人n. a retainer attached to a lord's household.
castration / 腐n. the palace punishment, 宫刑; 腐罪, an offence punished by it.
palace attendant / 给事中n. one attending within the palace.
officer / 吏n. a man in government service below the rank of official; a clerk or functionary.
eunuch / 宦者n. a castrated servant of the palace.
Commandery / 郡n. the largest territorial unit of a state, above the County.
the Six States / 六国n. Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan and Qi, the eastern states facing Qin.
ten thousand chariots / 万乘n. the measure of a great state's strength; a ruler of ten thousand chariots is a king.
Emperor / 帝n. the title above king, claimed by Qin and Qi in these years.
commoner / 布衣n. literally one in hempen clothes: a man without rank.
Court Gentleman / 郎n. a court attendant of the king's entourage; the office, as distinct from 士, the gentlemen as a class.
hegemon / 霸n. the lord who holds the leadership of the Lords.
the Five Hegemons / 五霸; 五伯n. the five lords who in turn led the states in the Spring and Autumn period.
east of the Pass / 关东n. the lands east of Hangu Pass, the eastern states.
County / 县n. the basic unit of direct administration, capitalized as an administrative unit.
swear the Vertical Alliance / 约从; 约纵v. to form the 合纵 (hezong) alliance, the north–south ("vertical") league of the eastern states against Qin; 从 stands for 纵.
Senior Clerk / 长史n. the chief secretary of a minister or office.
Guest Minister / 客卿n. a minister of foreign origin at the Qin court.
the Nine Yi / 九夷n. the Yi peoples of Chu's lands (Suoyin); a set phrase for the southern peoples.
the Vertical Alliance / 从; 纵n. the north–south league of the eastern states against Qin.
Your Majesty / 陛下n. the address to the ruler, literally "beneath the steps".
harem / 后宫n. the women's quarters of the palace, literally the "rear palace".
the Five Emperors / 五帝n. the sage rulers before the Three Kings.
the Three Kings / 三王n. the founders of the Xia, Shang and Zhou.
the black-headed people / 黔首n. the common people, the Qin term.
Commandant of Justice / 廷尉n. the chief judicial officer of Qin.
Emperor / 皇帝n. the title the King of Qin took in 221 BC.
Chancellor / 丞相n. the head of the administration; the fuller form of the title the texts also write as 相.
Fortress / 城n. a walled place.

Notes

1 third year247 BC, this year; the Basic Annals (§1) give the day, Bingwu of the fifth month. The Jijie cites Xu Guang: Zheng was then thirteen. The two Biographies run on from this point far past the year: the matter of Lao Ai falls in the king's early years and ends with Ai's revolt and death in 238 BC and Buwei's fall in 237 BC; Li Si's memorial is dated by the Zhengyi to the king's tenth year, 237 BC, and his Biography closes with the unification of 221 BC. The passages are given whole here, where the chronology meets them.
2 Second Father仲父: the Zhengyi: 仲 is 中, the middle; a second father; on the model of Duke Huan of Qi, who made Guan Zhong his 仲父.
3 the four lordsThe Zhengyi, from the Table: Lord Pingyuan died in King Zhaoxiang's fifty-sixth year (251 BC), Lord Xinling in the First Emperor's fourth (243 BC), and Li Yuan killed Lord Chunshen in his ninth (238 BC); Lord Mengchang died after King Zhaoxiang's twenty-fourth year (283 BC), the earliest of the four. The Suoyin cites Wang Shao, "Mengchang and Chunshen had long been dead", and answers that by the Table and the Biographies Mengchang and Pingyuan died somewhat earlier, but Xinling's command of the five states against Qin at Hewai falls in King Zhuangxiang's reign, when Buwei was already Chancellor, and Chunshen and Buwei were contemporaries, each Chancellor for over ten years, so that he cannot be said to have been long dead.
4 Eight Surveys八览、六论、十二纪: the Suoyin names the eight Surveys (有始, 孝行, 慎大, 先识, 审分, 审应, 离俗, 时君), the six Discourses (开春, 慎行, 贵直, 不苟, 以顺, 士容) and the twelve Records, one for each month, beginning with 孟春; the two hundred thousand-odd words make twenty-six juan.
5 Lüshi Chunqiu吕氏春秋: Master Lü's Spring and Autumn Annals, the compilation of Lü Buwei's retainers in twenty-six juan; quoted under 256 BC (§7).
6 XianyangThe Suoyin: the Dilizhi's Weicheng County in Youfufeng is the old Xianyang, renamed Xincheng by Emperor Gao and Weicheng by Emperor Jing; 咸 means "all", the place lying north of the Wei and south of the northern slope, on the 阳 side of both water and hill.
7 the First Emperor grew to manhood始皇帝: the Biography names the young king by his later title.
8 a wheel of tong wood桐轮: the Zhengyi: a small cart wheel made of tong wood.
9 castration腐罪: the Zhengyi: 腐 is read fu; the palace punishment, 宫刑, of the convict.
10 YongThe Zhengyi: the old city of Yong lies seven li south of Yong County in Qizhou, with the Da Zheng Palace of the Qin capital.
11 ShangcaiThe Suoyin: the Dilizhi's Shangcai County in Runan, "the old state of Cai, fief of Shu Du, King Wu of Zhou's brother; at the eighteenth generation Marquis Ping moved to Xincai", both in Runan; two generations later Marquis Zhao moved to Xiacai, in Pei; in the time of the Six States it was Chu land, hence "Shangcai of Chu".
12 petty officer of the Commandery郡小吏: the Suoyin: a petty clerk of a district 乡小史; Liu: in charge of the district's documents.
13 the travellers have the management of affairs游者主事: the Suoyin: in an age when the lords of ten thousand chariots contend for mastery, the travelling persuaders can win merit and fame and are given charge of affairs; Liu's reading, "travel among the Lords and seek a strong master to serve", twists the text and is wrong.
14 the harvest-time of the travelling persuaders秋: the Zhengyi: in autumn all things ripen; in a time of contention the persuaders too are ripe.
15 a beast staring at meat禽鹿视肉: the Suoyin: 禽鹿 means birds and beasts, which know only to stare at meat and eat it; the Zhuangzi and Master Su say "a man who does not learn is like one who stares at meat and eats", and Yang Xiong's Fayan "a man who does not learn, how does he differ from a beast?"; one who cannot persuade and win honour is as the beasts, having a man's face and able only to walk upright.
16 disgrace诟: the Zhengyi: read hou; shame.
17 carping at the age非世: the Suoyin: 非 is to censure, the "unbridled discourse of men out of office". The Zhengyi: to censure the age's wealth and rank, to hate its honours and gains and entrust oneself to inaction is not the gentleman's true feeling; it is that his strength cannot attain them.
18 King Zhuangxiang died247 BC: the point at which Li Si's story meets this year. What follows, his rise under King Zheng and the memorial of 237 BC, belongs to the following years.
19 The petty man胥人者，去其几也: the Suoyin: 胥人 is like 胥吏, petty men; 去 is to lose; 几 is the first stirring of movement; the gentleman sees the stirring and acts without waiting out the day, while the petty man does not know the moment of stirring and so always loses his time; Liu's gloss of 几 as "strong" is wrong. The Zhengyi reads 胥 as 相, "mutually", and 几 as 察, "to examine": the Six States east of the Pass stand against Qin as rivals, and the secret affairs of their rulers and ministers all have flaws by which great deeds may be achieved if one bears with them ruthlessly.
20 seizing on a flaw因瑕衅而遂忍之: the Suoyin: where the Lords have flaws, steel the heart and cut them away; "so I shall urge Qin to annex the world".
21 six reigns六世: the Zhengyi: Duke Xiao, Duke Huiwen, King Wu, King Zhao, King Xiaowen and King Zhuangxiang.
22 sweeping the dust off a stove灶上骚除: the Jijie cites Xu Guang, 骚 read as 埽; the Suoyin: Qin's annexing of the world would be like the cook sweeping the dirt from the stove top, no difficulty at all.
23 building an irrigation canal注溉渠: the Zhengyi: the Zheng Guo canal rose twenty-five li south-west of Yunyang County in Yongzhou, drawn from Zhongshan westward at Hukou, and ran along the northern hills east into the Luo, three hundred-odd li, to water the fields; Han, hard pressed by Qin's arms, sent the water engineer Zheng Guo to spy on Qin by building the canal, to waste its labour and keep it from attacking eastward.
24 all guests be expelled without exception一切逐客: the Suoyin: 一切 is "as one rule", to expel them all; 切 is as a sharp knife cutting, one stroke of the axe severing everything; those who gloss 一切 in the Hanshu as "for the time being" have not got it.
25 submitted a memorialThe Zhengyi: in the First Emperor's tenth year (237 BC). The memorial is the 谏逐客书, the remonstrance against the expulsion of the guests.
26 Duke Mu缪公: Duke Mu 穆公 as the memorial writes him.
27 Baili XiThe Suoyin: the Basic Annals of Qin say Duke Xian of Jin sent Baili Xi to Qin as a bride-servant with Duke Mu's wife; Xi fled to Wan, and rustics of Chu seized him. The Zhengyi cites the Xinxu: Baili Xi, a man of Wan in Chu, served in Yu; when Yu fell he entered Qin, and was called the Grand Master of the Five Rams' Skins.
28 Jian ShuThe Suoyin: the Basic Annals also have Baili Xi tell Duke Mu, "I am not the equal of my friend Jian Shu, who is worthy but unknown to the age", whereupon the duke welcomed him with rich gifts and made him Senior Grand Master; "from Song" has no known source. The Zhengyi cites the Kuodizhi: Jian Shu was a man of Qizhou, then travelling in Song, hence welcomed from Song.
29 Pi Bao and Gongsun ZhiThe Suoyin: Pi Bao's flight from Jin to Qin is plain in the Zuo Tradition; Gongsun Zhi is Zi Sang, a Grand Master of Qin, and his coming from Jin has no known source. The Zhengyi cites the Kuodizhi: Gongsun Zhi was a man of Qizhou who travelled in Jin and later returned to Qin.
30 annexed twenty states并国二十: the Suoyin: the Basic Annals say Duke Mu, on You Yu's counsel, attacked the king of the Rong, added twelve states and opened a thousand li of land, becoming hegemon over the Western Rong; here the merits of all five are summed, hence "twenty"; texts that alter it to "twelve" are wrong.
31 the plans of Zhang YiThe Suoyin: in King Hui's time Zhang Yi, as Chancellor, asked to attack Han and send troops down to Sanchuan to menace the two Zhou; Sima Cuo asked to attack Shu, the king agreed, and Shu was destroyed; after Yi's death King Wu wished to open the road to Sanchuan and had Gan Mao take Yiyang. All is credited to Zhang Yi here because he was Chancellor of Qin, and because he first asked to attack Sanchuan.
32 Shang CommanderyThe Zhengyi: in King Hui's tenth year (328 BC) Wei ceded the fifteen Counties of Shang Commandery.
33 HanzhongThe Zhengyi: in King Hui's thirteenth year (of his second reckoning, 312 BC) Qin attacked Chu's Hanzhong and took six hundred li of land.
34 Nine Yi九夷: the Suoyin: the Yi belonging to Chu; the Dilizhi's Jiangling County in Nan Commandery is "the old Ying, capital of Chu", and its Yicheng County "the old Yan". The Zhengyi: the Yi are the annexing of Ba and Shu, the gathering in of Shang Commandery, the taking of Hanzhong and the attacks on Yiqu and Danli; the Nine Yi are properly the nine kinds of Eastern Yi, and the phrase is a figure of style.
35 ChenggaoThe Zhengyi: Sishui County 氾水县 of Henan 河南府, in the names of the Tang.
36 Huayang华阳: Lord Huayang 华阳君, Mi Rong. The Jijie cites Xu Guang: 华 in one text 叶.
37 nibbled away蚕食: the Suoyin cites Gao You on the Huainanzi: to eat up, leaving nothing.
38 KunshanThe Zhengyi: Kungang lies four hundred li north-east of the state of Khotan (于阗), and the ridge yields jade.
39 treasures of Sui and He随、和之宝: the pearl of the Marquis of Sui and the jade disc of Bian He. The Zhengyi cites the Kuodizhi: Mount Fen, also called Kunshan and Duanshe Hill, "the hill of the severed snake", lies twenty-five li north of Sui County in Suizhou; the Shuoyuan: the Marquis of Sui on a journey met a great snake cut in two and, suspecting it was uncanny, had it dressed with medicine; the snake was able to go, and the place was named for it; after a year the snake brought him a bright pearl an inch across, pure white and shining, called the pearl of Sui. The disc of Bian He the First Emperor made the seal of state handed down.
40 Tai'e太阿: the Jijie: see the Biography of Su Qin. The Suoyin cites the Yuejueshu: the King of Chu summoned Ou Yezi and Gan Jiang to make three iron swords, Ganjiang, Moye and Tai'e.
41 Xianli纤离: the Jijie cites Xu Guang: Xianli and Pushao are both names of fine horses; the Suoyin: both horse names, Xu following the Sunqingzi.
42 drums of magic alligator hide灵鼍之鼓: the Jijie cites Zheng Xuan on the Yueling: alligator hide can be stretched over drums.
43 jueti駃騠: the Suoyin gives the reading jue-ti; the Zhoushu, "the true north offers jueti"; the Guangya, "a kind of horse"; Guo Pu on the Shanglin fu, "born three days and it outruns its mother".
44 Western Shu西蜀: Shu, the Chengdu plain.
45 the lower ranks下陈: the Suoyin: the rear ranks; Master Yan, "I have two daughters and wish to have them enter among the lower ranks".
46 hairpins of Wan pearls宛珠之簪，傅玑之珥: the Suoyin: 宛 read yuan, to wind pearls round the hairpin; 傅 read fu; 傅玑 is to set seed-pearls on the ear-pendant, 珥 being the ear-plug and 玑 a pearl that is not round; or 宛珠 is the pearl of Sui, Sui lying south of the Han and Wan near it; women's ornaments, none of them things Qin possesses.
47 E gauze阿缟: the Jijie cites Xu Guang: Dong'e County of Qi, whence silks come.
48 elegant and refined after the fashion随俗雅化: the Jijie cites Xu Guang, 随俗 in one text 修使; the Suoyin: refined and adaptable, able to suit the common taste.
49 Striking pots and beating jars击甕叩缶: the Suoyin cites the Shuowen: 甕 a water jar; 缶 an earthen vessel, which the men of Qin beat to keep time to music.
50 Zhao, Yu, Wu and Xiang昭、虞、武、象: the Jijie cites Xu Guang, 昭 in one text 韶; the dances of Shun, of King Wu and the Xiang.
51 Taishan太山: Mount Tai. The Suoyin cites the Guanzi, "the sea does not refuse water and so achieves its greatness; the mountain does not refuse earth and stones and so achieves its height", and the Wenzi, "the sage does not refuse the words of the firewood-carrier, and so widens his name".
52 supply资: the Suoyin: 给, to supply.
53 lending arms to the bandit藉寇兵而赍盗粮: the Suoyin gives the readings and cites the Shuowen, 赍 is to carry and present; 赍 or 资, the sense the same.
54 restored Li Si to his officeThe Jijie cites the Xinxu: Si, among those expelled, submitted his memorial of remonstrance on the road; it reached the First Emperor, who sent men after him as far as Liyi and brought him back.
55 In twenty-odd yearsThe unification of 221 BC and the imperial title; the Biography's summary runs to the end of the First Emperor's reign.
